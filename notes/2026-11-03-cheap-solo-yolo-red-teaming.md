## Long summary

[@otaliptus](https://x.com/otaliptus) describes a two-month, budget-conscious method for solo defensive scanning of repositories—especially in Bitcoin and nearby open-source systems—and how they choose models and platforms when they cannot keep up with every new model, hack, abliteration, or company. They say even well-configured agents still leave a human to judge whether a tool is useful, and that a typical reader with far less screen time cannot track changing cyber practice. They date a shift in seriousness to around the release of Kimi K3 (they think earlier models may count, but are not sure): two 8-figure thefts, daily new attacks, and a Binance incident they put at 6 billion. Until about a month before writing, many people in AI were skeptical that OSS models would matter for hacks; the author argues American frontier models can also be pushed into harmful help if someone is stubborn enough, and that results are now visible. They name php2shell, a CA cert 4 bug (they say this one maybe does not count), and brotli as examples, then claim zero-days are appearing in operating systems, firmware, and obscure hardware—some unpatchable in the sense of Spectre and Meltdown, some human error, some expected intentional backdoors. Modern protocols, software, and cryptography are treated as potentially in danger, with no pause in pace.

With volunteer engineers they scanned hundreds to thousands of repositories and recorded more than 10k potential bug/vuln findings. A large portion was evaluated, sent, received, and patched; some disclosures were still in flight. The rest of the piece is how they scan when the budget is not large.

**Taste.** Any model is usable for a repository scan if it does not cut responses short or refuse. Do not get distracted by obscure or weak models; scan.

**Cyber capabilities are not software-engineering capabilities.** Most public benchmarks are SWE-focused; the few cyber benchmarks are, in their view, not good enough. This matters for Chinese models: they are good at cyber, not that good at SWE (at least 3 months behind American models, maybe 6). Persistence in looking for bugs is a reason to use them anyway.

**Size.** A home B70 running small/mid Qwens is only for hobbies and more private work. For cyber they want a large, “tasty” model that stays smart, does not loop mid-check, and does not invent fake criticals. They say Qwen 3.8 27B Uncensored is not the model for a cyber scan even if finetuned on cyber data, and that Qwen 3.8 Flash Next is also useless beyond the most obvious bugs. Recommended names: Deepseek Flash, Kimi, GLM, Mimo, Minimax (they then say maybe do not use Minimax; they call it pure benchmaxxing), Claude, Codex, Google (if access exists), Grok, and Meta. Use something large that can use tools under ordinary harnesses. As of October 2026 they advise against spending time or money on setups that will not work.

**Start with Chinese models, carefully.** They put Chinese models 4–6 months behind frontier US models (they dismiss LLM-as-a-judge / benchmaxxing). On cyber they may be closer, about 2–3 months, because American models refuse more. They are cheap: bulky Kimi may not be the cheapest; GLM is fairly priced; MiMo 2.6 Pro is underestimated; MiMo 2.6 Flash is much cheaper and strong on cyber; Deepseek v4.1 Flash (October 17 release) is cheap, smart, and capable, though a bit more eager to wrap up than the previous version. Run them often as a first check. If API cost is a concern, OpenCode Go is cited at 10 dollars a month for hard-to-finish Deepseek usage for a solo developer; they say it will not refuse audits of one’s own repositories and systems (they add that one should not use it against other people’s systems). Chinese models are framed as a cheap first pass that can produce a usable report.

**Check Chinese outputs with American models, and scan with those too.** They call Chinese output heavy slop: of 30 findings they would bet 15 are fake, 5 useless, and 10 real with overstated impact—and that those 10 still often include 2–3 highs/crits. Filtering that list with American models is how they make the work tractable.

With KYC they “yolo” Mythos 5.1 (under Anthropic CVP) and 5.6 Sol Daybreak Blue (they say Red is not given to ordinary users; it exists for security teams of S&P 500 companies). They run Mythos and Daybreak Blue on high-xhigh on a personal subscription. Mythos 5.1 is generally sensible; on one’s own repository, Opus 5.5 under CVP generally does not reject, and inserting a finding in a longer chat to check validity works. They say Mythos never rejects unless the work is auditing ECDSA itself or attacking Satoshi’s coins. On GPT, even with Daybreak Blue, GPT-6 models (Astra, Sol, Terra) reject cyber-related work; the latest Daybreak model is GPT-6 Sol. Their advice is not to bother: GPT 5.6 is still good enough for many defensive scans and also rejects ECDSA-itself attacks. A Claude-side note: Haiku 5.5 (no KYC) has almost zero cyber refusals and is not a super-dumb model. Without KYC, 5.6 Sol was/is still usable but harder to steer. They mention finding a Coldcard bug without Daybreak (they were KYC’ed). They tell readers not to use a Codex security workflow plugin that GPT models like during audits: it burns a subscription’s tokens in an hour and the quality is only okay versus a regular single-thread message.

**Abliteration and ZDR.** For most defensive scanning they do not think abliterated models are needed. Those models are more relevant if someone is pentesting or attacking something that should be their own. Two fears: altered weights might produce strange behavior, including secretly uploading credentials or audited projects to a cloud bucket, and many abliterated-model providers are small or little-known; and they are unsure those providers have proper zero-data-retention, whereas they treat OpenAI and Claude as too large to immediately mishandle data even if ZDR is imperfect. Combined with work on critical Bitcoin infrastructure and critical open source, they urge caution, a real sandbox (self-built, or examples they name: Trail of Bits, Vercel, even Docker) so a runaway model cannot reach the auditor’s environment, and care about what is shared. They say not to do unethical work (their example: trying to access Satoshi’s coins).

**KYC.** They did KYC for OpenAI and Anthropic in order to use frontier models for audits. Skipping KYC is fine: an OpenCode subscription at 10 dollars, or an upper tier at 40 dollars, is enough to clear many easy bugs. They think attackers mostly use the same cheap tools—open-source Chinese models or abliterated open-source Chinese models—rather than something like Mythos, because Anthropic is “overly paranoid,” though they are not sure. Chinese models need money, not KYC (OpenCode or API). The refrain: keep scanning.

**API.** Competition-style agent swarms with skills can find more bugs or exploit chains, but for ordinary defensive protection they do not think heavy API spend is required. Subsidized subscriptions cover basic work. Swarms also produce more slop; more tokens raise the chance of findings, but not linearly. With unlimited money, hitting the API and coordinating about ten agents with Opus, Mythos, or similar is fair. Chinese LLM APIs are relatively cheap; platforms named from memory are [@baseten](https://x.com/baseten), [@FireworksAI_HQ](https://x.com/FireworksAI_HQ), [@TryMapleAI](https://x.com/TryMapleAI), [@BoundlessHQ](https://x.com/BoundlessHQ), and https://numes.ai, plus many others. On a tighter budget they describe 200 dollars of GPT and 200 dollars of Claude per month, plus 10 or 40 dollars of OpenCode, or a couple of dollars for a carefully run abliterated model. During their red teaming, total API spend was not even a thousand dollars; personal subscriptions did most of the work. They say you do not need 5,000 dollars per month to protect against simpler bugs; many audited repositories had easy-to-find issues. Excluding the Liquid hack—which they say LLMs also found in a not-so-costly way—most of the worst bugs were easy to find. They do not think you need to be rich to red-team your own or friends’ repositories; even 500 dollars per month is more than enough.

**Harness.** Harness quality matters, but a working regular setup is enough: Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Pi, OMP, or something of your own. A common mistake (more in ordinary development than in cyber) is flooding the model with skills so it burns tokens. They still find a vanilla setup useful if instructions are clear. One or two skills is fine; they advise against burying the run in skills and AGENTS.md files. Many of their scans used plain Codex, plain Claude Code, or even GPT webUI.

**Responsible disclosure and quality.** After a scan that produces many categorized bugs, drop slop before reporting. Read the reports; 5.6 Sol is called strong at cheap filtering. For high or critical issues, try to produce proof-of-concept demonstrations. False positives are common; re-auditing across models and having models read each other’s reports and PoCs helps. Chinese models produce more slop; American frontier models (Mythos and 5.6, in their experience) generally will audit those reports. Give maintainers time; write a readable report rather than a “slop cannon.” ASD STE 100 is named as something that can make writing easier. Include proof-of-concept code, why the bug exists, and a likelihood field. They warn about silly reports: bugs that only appear if a library API is used in a ridiculous way, or only if (for example) a chip’s entropy path fails—if normal conditions assume working hardware, they treat that as a boundary condition. Another example: an RPC finding that an operator can create infinite money when the endpoint is behind an API key or internal gateway. Access assumptions matter. Double- or triple-pass the report; ideally a human looks at it; otherwise still get reviews, including agents talking to each other. They suggest four terminals and about five minutes of reading. Respect maintainer process. For maintainers: provide a place to report security issues (they once had to use LinkedIn, which they did not like) and do not treat good-faith reporters badly.

**Maths.** Auditing something like ECDSA or other cryptography has two problems. First, many models cannot do it. Chinese models are “really bad at maths”; on an MPC implementation they have seen 60 findings of which 55 were nonsense, across multiple cryptographic libraries. Those models can find some implementation bugs but not audit the maths. For that they want frontier models. Claude is still second place; Fable and Opus are impressive but better at implementation checks than mathematical checks. GPT models are better at maths. Second, how to use GPT: Max/Ultra costs many tokens, or use Pro models in the webUI (they worry that path may be withdrawn). GPT 5.6 Pro used on cryptographic libraries is called amazing: it can run about two hours, generally does not reject, and produces mathematical audits with unusual maths bugs they could report; almost nobody called those findings nonsense except a few API-usage cases. They report successful disclosures to a couple of cryptographic libraries with GPT 5.6 Pro. GPT-6 Pro Astra is even better in their view but often rejects. On the webUI, GPT-6 xhigh rejects much less than GPT-6 Pro, is good at maths (sometimes comparable to 5.6 Pro), and is much faster. They run two or three queries at once: 5.6 Pro, 6 Pro, and 6 Xhigh; 6 Pro generally rejects while the other two do not. If 6 Pro already produced artifacts, logs, code, or partial results before stopping, continuing with 6 xhigh can recover useful points. Comparing and combining those answers has been useful. webUI GPT models helped on low-level maths: curve implementations, hashing functions, signing, or potential nonce reuse. 5.6 Pro is strong but slow. On libraries used by millions of people, almost all maintainers said the findings made sense; most were medium at best, with a couple of highs and one critical. Lows and mediums can combine, especially in a library. Takeaway: do not use Chinese models for the maths itself. For low-hanging fruit, start with Opus 5.5, Mythos 5.1, or maybe Sonnet. For more serious cryptographic checks they prefer 5.6 Pro, 6 xhigh, 6 Ultra, or ideally 6 Pro with Astra when a response arrives (about half the time for them). 5.6 Pro generally does not reject.

**TL;DR they give.** Scan everything you have every week with every model in budget. For cyber, usefulness order: Mythos 5.1 (KYC) > 5.6 Sol Daybreak Blue (KYC) >>> others for double-checking findings; Mythos 5.1 (KYC) >= generally non-refusal models that produce good results plus slop (DS v4.1 Flash, MiMo 2.6 Pro/Flash, GLM 5.3, Kimi K3.1, Mistral Large 4, Grok 4.7) = 5.6 Sol Daybreak Blue (KYC) (less slop). If Opus 5.5 can be made to work in the repo for bugs, that is the best scenario. GPT-6 Astra is hopeless for cyber checks; GPT-6 Sol is “useless trash”; 6.1 Sol is not super smart; use 5.6 Sol Daybreak Blue. 5.6 Pro is underrated for hard math and complex audits. Do not use the Codex Security plugin unless you want to burn a weekly quota or spend 1000 USD in one go. For any High/Crit, try to have a PoC. You do not need thousands of dollars; Chinese models via OpenCode Go or providers can be enough. A model with taste that is big enough does not need a week of harness design. You do not need many subagents for the low-hanging bugs attackers commonly chase; deeper work may need multi-agent swarms and more money. American models are a bit better; Chinese models are also capable. Spend tokens within budget. A Linux-kernel-scale target needs more tokens; starting does not. Closing line: Scan. Patch. Repeat. Enjoy.

## Key claims

- The author spent about two months red-teaming the Bitcoin ecosystem and other places they could be useful, with volunteer engineers scanning hundreds to thousands of repositories and recording more than 10k potential bug/vuln findings, many of which were evaluated, reported, and patched.
- They date a visible rise in AI-related cyber impact to around Kimi K3 (possibly earlier; they are not sure), citing two 8-figure thefts, ongoing compromises, and a Binance hack they describe as 6 billion, plus named incidents php2shell, a CA cert 4 bug, and brotli.
- They treat modern protocols, software, and cryptography as potentially in danger; some future issues may be unpatchable in the Spectre/Meltdown sense, and some may be intentional backdoors.
- Model “taste” matters less than not being cut off or refused; do not get stuck on obscure or weak models—scan.
- Cyber skill is not the same as SWE skill; most benchmarks are SWE-focused and the few cyber ones are not good enough. Chinese models are good at cyber and at least 3 months (maybe 6) behind American models on SWE; on cyber they may be only 2–3 months behind because American models refuse more.
- Small/local setups (home B70, small/mid Qwens, Qwen 3.8 27B Uncensored, Qwen 3.8 Flash Next) are not what they use for serious cyber scans, even if finetuned; they want large models: Deepseek Flash, Kimi, GLM, Mimo, Claude, Codex, Google, Grok, Meta (and they caution against Minimax as benchmaxxing). Advice is as of October 2026.
- Chinese models are the cheap first pass: GLM fairly priced; MiMo 2.6 Pro underestimated; MiMo 2.6 Flash much cheaper and strong on cyber; Deepseek v4.1 Flash (October 17 release) cheap, smart, a bit more eager to wrap up than the previous version. OpenCode Go at 10 dollars a month is cited for solo Deepseek use on one’s own repos.
- Chinese first-pass output is heavy slop: of 30 findings they expect about 15 fake, 5 useless, 10 real with overstated impact, of which 2–3 are often highs/crits. Filter and re-scan with American models.
- With KYC they use Mythos 5.1 (Anthropic CVP) and 5.6 Sol Daybreak Blue on high-xhigh (Red not offered to ordinary users). Mythos 5.1 is generally sensible; Opus 5.5 under CVP generally does not reject on one’s own repo. Mythos rejects ECDSA-itself / Satoshi’s-coins work. GPT-6 Astra, Sol, and Terra reject cyber work; use GPT 5.6 / 5.6 Sol Daybreak Blue instead. Haiku 5.5 (no KYC) has almost zero cyber refusals. Do not use the Codex security workflow plugin (burns a subscription in about an hour; quality only okay).
- Abliterated models are usually unnecessary for defensive scanning; the author worries about strange behavior from altered weights (including leaking credentials or projects) and unclear ZDR at small providers. Use a sandbox (self-built, Trail of Bits, Vercel, or Docker).
- KYC for OpenAI and Anthropic is how they reached frontier models; without KYC, OpenCode at 10 or 40 dollars plus Chinese APIs is enough for many easy bugs. They think attackers are more likely to use open-source or abliterated Chinese models than Mythos.
- Heavy API spend and agent swarms help competitions and deeper exploit-chain hunting but add slop; subscriptions can cover ordinary defense. Named cheap API platforms: [@baseten](https://x.com/baseten), [@FireworksAI_HQ](https://x.com/FireworksAI_HQ), [@TryMapleAI](https://x.com/TryMapleAI), [@BoundlessHQ](https://x.com/BoundlessHQ), and https://numes.ai. Example budget: 200 dollars GPT + 200 dollars Claude + 10 or 40 dollars OpenCode. Their own API spend was under 1,000 dollars. They say 5,000 dollars per month is unnecessary for simpler bugs; 500 dollars per month is more than enough. The Liquid hack is noted as also found by LLMs at modest cost.
- Harness: Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Pi, OMP, or a reliable personal setup is enough. Do not flood the model with skills or AGENTS.md files; many scans were plain Codex, Claude Code, or GPT webUI.
- Before disclosure: remove slop (5.6 Sol is named as a cheap filter), require PoCs for high/crit, cross-check models, write readable reports (ASD STE 100 mentioned), include PoC, cause, and likelihood, and respect maintainer time. Watch false assumptions (ridiculous API use, hardware-failure entropy, RPC endpoints behind API keys or internal gateways). Maintainers should publish a security-reporting channel.
- For cryptographic maths (e.g. ECDSA, MPC), Chinese models are poor (example: 60 findings, 55 nonsense). Claude / Fable / Opus are better at implementation than maths. GPT is better at maths. GPT 5.6 Pro can run about two hours, generally does not reject, and supported successful disclosures (mostly medium, a couple of highs, one critical). GPT-6 Pro Astra is stronger but often rejects (~half the time they get a response). GPT-6 xhigh rejects less than GPT-6 Pro and is faster. They run 5.6 Pro, 6 Pro, and 6 Xhigh together and may continue a stopped 6 Pro thread with 6 xhigh. Low-hanging fruit: Opus 5.5, Mythos 5.1, or Sonnet. Serious crypto: 5.6 Pro, 6 xhigh, 6 Ultra, or 6 Pro with Astra.
- Weekly scan-with-what-you-can-afford is the program: Mythos 5.1 (KYC) then 5.6 Sol Daybreak Blue (KYC) for double-checking; otherwise DS v4.1 Flash, MiMo 2.6 Pro/Flash, GLM 5.3, Kimi K3.1, Mistral Large 4, Grok 4.7; Opus 5.5 is best if it works in-repo; avoid GPT-6 Astra / GPT-6 Sol / 6.1 Sol for cyber; skip the Codex Security plugin unless you intend to burn quota or about 1000 USD; always try a PoC on High/Crit. ## Actionables

- Scan your own repositories on a weekly cadence with every model that fits the budget, then patch and repeat.
- Use a large model for cyber review; do not treat small local or uncensored Qwen-class setups as sufficient for that job.
- Run cheap Chinese models (for example via OpenCode Go or the named API platforms) as a first pass on your own code, then filter the report with American frontier models rather than filing the first list.
- Prefer subsidized subscriptions over heavy API swarms unless you are hunting deeper than common low-hanging bugs; the author treats about 500 dollars per month as more than enough for ordinary self-protection.
- Keep the harness simple (plain Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Pi, OMP, or GPT webUI) and avoid skill/plugin piles; skip the Codex security workflow plugin if token budget matters.
- Before you disclose: drop slop, try to attach a PoC for high/critical items, cross-check across models, state likelihood and access assumptions, and give maintainers time. If you maintain software, publish a security-reporting contact.
- For defensive scanning, default to non-abliterated models and run audits in a sandbox. Do not point this workflow at other people’s systems.
- For cryptographic or mathematical review, do not rely on Chinese models for the maths; the author points to GPT 5.6 Pro / GPT-6 xhigh / related Pro-class runs, and to Opus 5.5, Mythos 5.1, or Sonnet for shallower implementation checks.
